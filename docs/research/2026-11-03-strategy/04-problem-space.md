# The problem space: 15 candidates, one decision

> **Revised scoring.** The first pass ranked the follow-up loop first (91.0) and the closed-loop results second (85.4). On re-check, the follow-up category is crowded and the result loop is the stronger white space. The revised recommendation is [the result accountability loop](13-revised-recommendation-result-loop.md). The scoring below is the first pass, not the final ranking.

This note is the longlist and the scoring. The recommendation is in `05-recommended-thesis.md`. The scoring is a judgment, not a measurement; the weights and the reasons are explicit so the team can disagree with the inputs rather than the conclusion.

## Why A wins

### It has the strongest Indian evidence

- A Chennai audit found only 11.2% of 3,696 patients were reliably trackable by phone and address; 34.4% had missing or illegible contact information; missing contact was associated with pre-treatment loss to follow-up (OR 4.5).
- A Punjab study found 50 to 60% of public hypertension patients missed scheduled follow-up; the top reasons were no symptoms, distance and no instructions.
- A Seoni QI cut missed visits from 66% to 22% in nine months with ASHA home visits and record triangulation.
- Cancer abandonment is measured at 14.3% (AIIMS/CanKids) and 27.1% (Assam).
- Diabetic retinopathy delay is measured at 50.5% "eyes okay", 41% no attender, 38.6% cost, with 26% progressing.

No other candidate has this density of Indian numerators.

### It is the only candidate where the KPI is both obvious and movable

Recall completion, reachability rate, barrier resolution, unreached rate, staff minutes. A 60 to 90 day pilot can measure all five. The Seoni result proves the operational loop can move the KPI without a new clinical intervention.

### It fits the clinic's actual information infrastructure

Paper registers, Excel, WhatsApp, shared phones, partial addresses. The product does not require an EMR or ABDM integration to start. It meets the clinic where it is, which is exactly the programme's design bar.

### It works across private and government, and down the tiers

- Private clinic: recovered visits are revenue; the owner-doctor can buy.
- Government facility: loss to follow-up is a programme KPI; ASHA/ANM are the field arm; the district can buy.
- Tier 2/3/rural: the problem is worse there, and the product uses the channels that exist (phone, WhatsApp, ASHA visits, paper).
- Tier 1: the same product works with a digital front desk, but it is not the beachhead.

### It has a real, non-obvious mechanism

The insight is not "send reminders". It is: **the list is unreachable, and the reason patients do not return is a barrier, not forgetfulness.** The product makes the list reachable, asks why, routes the barrier, and proves every due patient has a disposition. That is a different job from a reminder app, and it is defensible as an operating layer.

### The AI has a clear job

- Read paper registers and reports to build the due list.
- Normalise, validate and dedupe phone numbers and addresses.
- Converse in the patient's language to find the barrier.
- Classify barriers and route them.
- Prioritise the list and generate the ASHA/field worklist.
- Draft the clinic brief and the audit.

The AI is not a chatbot bolted onto a list. It is the only way to do this at low cost across languages and messy paper.

## Why B does not win

B (closed-loop results) has higher novelty, a stronger safety story and a clearer "receipt" mechanism. It loses on three practical counts:

1. **No Indian baseline.** The best evidence is a US systematic review with a 6.8 to 62% range. The team would have to build the baseline in the pilot, which weakens the pitch.
2. **Order capture is the hard part.** Most small clinics do not record orders digitally. The product would have to start from a prescription photo, which adds friction before the loop begins.
3. **Willingness to pay is weaker.** Recall recovers revenue; results management prevents harm. Private clinics pay for the first more readily than the second. Hospitals and accreditors pay for the second, but the sales cycle is longer.

B remains the strongest second product, because it reuses the extraction pipeline and the audit layer. The recommendation is to build A first and add B as the second loop once the clinic trusts the ledger.

## Why the others lose

- **C (referral receipt)** is high severity and a strong government story, but the buyer is a programme or a hospital, the sales cycle is long, and the product needs a two-sided network. It is a good expansion after A, especially in cancer.
- **D (claims)** has the highest willingness to pay and a real AI job, but it is not a doctor's workflow; it is the admin/TPA desk's. It also carries the most regulatory and ethical complexity. It is a different company.
- **F (WhatsApp inbox)** is broad and tempting, but it is crowded, it is a feature more than a job, and it puts the riskiest data (unstructured patient messages) in the centre before the trust layer exists.
- **H (OPD flow)** is real but crowded; the product would compete with queue-management vendors and hospital IT. The AI adds little.
- **E (identity)** is infrastructure. It is a real need, but it is not a felt pain a doctor will pay for first. It is a good internal capability, not a beachhead.

## The hardest assumption for each top candidate

| Candidate | Hardest assumption | How to test it cheaply |
|---|---|---|
| A | Clinics will pay to fix reachability and resolve barriers, not just to send reminders | 10 interviews; ask for the register; run a 2-week manual worklist; ask for a paid continuation |
| B | Clinics will capture orders and act on a result ledger | Shadow 3 clinics; count how orders are recorded; ask who owns the result inbox |
| C | A sending doctor or programme will pay for a referral receipt | 5 oncologists; ask what happens today when a referral is lost; ask for a paid pilot |
| D | A hospital will pay for claim assembly and the doctor will cooperate | 3 hospital admins; ask for rejection rates and staff cost |
| H | A clinic will change scheduling behaviour, not just buy software | Observe 3 OPDs; measure no-show and walk-in mix; ask what they tried |

## What would change the recommendation

- If 7 of 10 clinics say they already have a reliable follow-up list and the problem is not reachability, A weakens.
- If clinics will not share or fix the register, the reachability wedge is unworkable; B or C becomes stronger.
- If the government pilot cannot be accessed within 90 days, the "private + government" claim is unproven; the beachhead becomes private only.
- If a well-funded incumbent already sells the exact reachability + barrier loop in India, A's novelty collapses; the fallback is B or C.

The validation plan in `06-validation-plan.md` turns these into experiments with kill criteria.
