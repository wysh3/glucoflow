# Jury chair — scorecard

Judged 4 November 2026 against PANEL_BRIEF.md §6–§10. Twenty-plus health finals, most of them Indian, most of them with two clinicians and one engineer on the panel. I have watched a better product lose to a worse one because the worse one had a moment, and I have watched a defensible company lose because its founder answered "who pays?" with a slide. I score the competition, not the abstract company: the §8 rubric is a proxy for a room that votes on recognition and trust, this programme splits ₹54+ lakh three ways across tracks and awards every finalist, so placement is worth less than being the team the room can still describe at lunch.

**T1 wins, and it wins on the only two things a live finale actually rewards — a refusal the room can watch and an artefact a doctor can file — not on the accreditation narrative the team keeps leading with.**

## Why the winner wins (top 3 reasons, evidence-backed)

**1. It is the only thesis whose unit is an obligation a named human owns, and the failure it fixes has a date.** A missed recheck has no event — you cannot point at the day it happened. A potassium that reached a receptionist does. §5 rows 3, 4 and 5 put the frequency beyond argument (10.7% of critical notifications unsuccessful; 64.61% notified 30–120 minutes after sample arrival with outpatient notification the weak point; 6.8–62% of abnormal ambulatory results not followed up), and both doctors in that room will have a scar they can date to a month. That is the difference between a problem the panel *agrees* with and a problem the panel *remembers*. T2 has the better statistic and the weaker memory: nobody can tell you when a follow-up did not happen.

**2. It is the only demo in the pack that contains both a visible refusal and a fileable artefact.** The server refuses a wrong value echo; a critical result cannot be marked patient-informed without a read-back; an unreachable close requires two documented attempts; and the certificate still prints, saying "not informed" rather than inventing a conversation. The rubric rewards a visible limit and a memorable artefact, and T1 has both inside forty seconds — and both survive a dim projector, a hostile wifi and a judge who has already clicked the seeded row. Nothing else in the pack is that robust to venue failure: T2 needs a live WhatsApp surface, T4 needs OCR to behave, T5 needs nothing at all, which is its whole problem.

**3. It is the only thesis that assembles a 2–1 coalition without splitting the pitch.** Doctor 1 recognises the scar. Doctor 2 trusts the boundary: the lab flag is the source of criticality, the product never derives it, there is no interpretation, and the refusal is the control. The technologist sees a server-enforced state machine, an append-only event table and a hash chain — a system, not a form. T2 wins one doctor and loses the technologist ("this is a CRM"). T5 wins the technologist and loses both doctors. T4 wins one doctor and loses the other on personal liability. T3 loses both. In a three-person panel, two beats one every time, and T1 is the only thesis that gets two without asking either judge to swallow something.

## Fatal flaw of each thesis (one line each)

- **T1 — Closure Ledger.** The buyer is a quality officer and the user is a doctor, and the receipt as built puts fourteen-odd interactions on the doctor for one critical result, so the liability shield becomes a second unpaid job on the days she is busiest — and the first screen a careless presenter shows is a strip of six statistic tiles, which is the dashboard the panel was told to punish.
- **T2 — 90-Day Recheck.** It is Setu with a diabetes register, in a programme that already contains Setu, and its one real differentiator ("we start with the patients nobody can reach") takes longer to explain than a judge's patience.
- **T3 — Annual Review Pack.** Completion requires the ophthalmologist, the nephrologist and the lab to stamp the pack, so the path breaks precisely where the evidence is strongest (§5 row 2: 89.9% of diabetics who knew their diagnosis had never had a retinal exam), and the honest demo is a checklist of the care that did not happen.
- **T4 — Bag Reconciliation.** Extraction *is* the product, so the first bad OCR in front of a doctor is unrecoverable, and "detect stopped drugs and impossible dates" walks the treatment-recommendation line the programme excludes in terms.
- **T5 — Identity Firewall.** There is no screen — nothing to open twice, nothing a doctor asks for — so the best engineering in the pack is aimed at a buyer who is not in the room and a moment that lasts one dialog box.

## What would make the winner 95+

1. **Move the typing off the doctor.** The nurse or front desk records the call, the channel and the patient's words; the doctor's surface is one tap to confirm the value plus the refusal. That single change answers the one question that will sink the current build in a live Q&A, and it costs a role, not a rewrite.
2. **One measured baseline from one site before 28 November.** "One hundred consecutive abnormal reports at one clinic: twenty-three had no documented acknowledgement." One real number from one real register beats the entire accreditation narrative, and the team currently has zero. This is the highest-value four days of work left.
3. **A real printed page.** A register that exists only behind a vendor login is worth nothing to a surveyor or a district file. Print it on stage and hand it to a judge. That is the artefact, and it is the thing the room will photograph.
4. **Attribution after acknowledgement, not before.** Do not stamp a named doctor overdue for a report that landed at 18:40 while she was in theatre. That is the difference between a shield and a noose, and the doctor judges will test for it.
5. **A second, untouched receipt and the open public-verifier tab.** Assume the first judge to reach the laptop closes the demo row. The verifier needs no login and cannot be consumed.
6. **Fix the opening number.** The demo script opens on "only one in four critical values reached the treating clinician" (25.8%). That figure is not in the judges' fact pack — the pack's rows 3 and 4 say 10.7% unsuccessful and 64.61% notified 30–120 minutes. Every judge reads the pack first. A number that is not in it reads as invented even when it is sourced elsewhere; either get it into the shared pack or lead with 64.61%.

## The one question I would ask at the finale

**"What does your product refuse to do, and what happens to the patient when it refuses?"**

It is the unbluffable question. A team that has built a limit can answer in ten seconds and will show you the screen. A team that has built a demo will describe accuracy.

## Verdict (one sentence)

Award T1: it is the only thesis where the room watches a machine refuse a doctor, a clinic admit it could not reach a patient, and a certificate print anyway — a demo a doctor recognises, a KPI that moves, a limit made visible, and an artefact somebody can file.

## Confidence and what I could not verify

High confidence on panel dynamics, stagecraft and differentiation, because I have watched this room behave the same way for a decade: two clinicians reward recognition and then trust, one engineer rewards substance, and the room remembers one moment and one sentence.

Medium confidence on buyer and price. I read the pack's §3 and §10, which are explicit that no customer, buyer interview, willingness-to-pay test, purchase order or pilot exists. I have not spoken to a quality head. The ₹5,000–20,000/facility/month and the ₹60,000/90-day lab pilot are unvalidated prices, and I have treated them as hypotheses throughout.

Low confidence, and it is the honest hole: **whether a doctor will tolerate the receipt at all.** The fourteen-interaction count is a count of built fields, not a timed usability study. If it is right, T1's adoption risk is larger than its scores here suggest; if the capture moves to a nurse role, the risk mostly disappears. That is why it is item one above.

I could not verify the 25.8% critical-value figure against the fact pack, the deployed product at finale scale, or any claim of pilot evidence — there is none.

## Appendix: the eight questions a jury chair owes the room

### 1. Which thesis wins this specific programme, given its published design bar and a two-doctor-one-technologist panel?

**T1, and the bar is being misread by anyone who answers T2.**

The design bar has two halves. Its *named KPI examples* — recall completion, no-show rate — point straight at T2, which is why a literal reading picks the wrong thesis. Its *shape* — assistive not diagnostic, human override and audit trail, light integration, low-cost channel, low digital fluency, 60–90 day operational proof — is a description of an administrative receipt with a human in the loop, and T1 is the purest instance of that shape in the pack. T1 also carries the only regulatory sentence in the pack that a clinician can repeat without qualification: the lab flag is the source of criticality, we do not derive it, we do not diagnose, we do not triage, we do not score risk; the clinic is the fiduciary and we are the processor. §5 row 13's CDSCO guidance and row 10's NABH read-back requirement make that sentence true rather than merely cautious.

Then the panel arithmetic. Two doctors vote on recognition and trust; one technologist votes on substance. T1 is the only thesis that gets two of three without a split pitch, and the second doctor's vote comes from the refusal, not from the architecture. A chairman's rule: at a live finale, the winner is usually the team that made the panel feel *safe* and *seen* in the same four minutes. T1 does both.

The honest counter-case, which I have weighed and rejected: T2 is a better fit to the bar's letter, has the cleaner buyer arithmetic (recovered visits are revenue, so the clinic can do the ROI itself), and is the only thesis whose KPI a judge can restate in one sentence. It loses on the entry ticket the brief names in §1 — differentiation — because Setu is in this same programme with the same thesis, and Clinizy, Yuktii (free), Lifemaan, Eka and Vitaloop sit on it commercially. A well-executed version of a taken idea wins the politeness prize.

### 2. What is the 40-second moment the room will remember, for each thesis?

**T1 — the refusal.** Open the K 6.1 receipt with the clock red and 4:00 left. The doctor types 6.9 against the printed 6.1 and the server returns: the printed value does not match the lab report; this receipt cannot be acknowledged. Two seconds of silence. Then 6.1, the echo, the lab caller, the read-back, the chain seals, the certificate prints. Second beat, same forty seconds if the presenter is disciplined: close a *different* receipt as "patient unreachable after two documented attempts" and let the certificate print anyway, saying "not informed". The room remembers two sentences: *it said no to a doctor*, and *it admitted the patient was never reached*.

**T2 — the patient's own sentence.** A Roman-Hindi reply arrives: "nahi aa paungi, koi saath le jane wala nahi hai." The agent does not send a reminder. It offers a video consult; she says yes; the row turns. The room remembers a product that listened, in her language, and did not blame her for not coming.

**T3 — the pack in the patient's hand.** Four of six boxes empty; the doctor's thumb on the retinal box that has been empty for fourteen months. Nothing happens on screen and everything is visible. It is the only artefact in the pack the patient carries out of the room.

**T4 — the bag, emptied.** Thirty-four objects on the table; forty seconds later one signed page says: taking two of the five prescribed, one duplicate, last HbA1c from a different lab and fourteen months old. The room remembers the physical bag and the number 2-of-5.

**T5 — the refusal to match.** Two Sunita Sharmas, one shared son's phone, two clinic IDs; the system refuses both candidates and asks one question that resolves it. The room remembers a machine declining to guess. It is also, honestly, a dialog box — forty seconds of trust with nothing around it.

### 3. Where will the judges' first skeptical question land, and which thesis answers it best?

The **first** question in an Indian health final is almost never about AI. It is: **"who does the work, and how long does it take them?"** Because both clinicians are running the arithmetic of their own OPD while you talk.

- **T2 answers it best.** The clerk already calls; the product is the clerk's list, script and disposition. Nobody new is hired and the doctor's day does not change. That is also why it is unexciting.
- **T1 answers it worst as built** — the doctor types. It survives only if the capture is role-split, and a good team will say so before being asked.
- **T4 answers it well** ("sixty seconds, one page, you sign it") and then loses the second question on liability.
- **T3 and T5 do not get asked**, because neither produces a felt change in the doctor's day.

The **second** question lands on the device boundary: "is this a medical device / are you diagnosing?" **T1 answers it best** and can name the CDSCO circular; T4 answers it worst, because "detects stopped drugs and duplicate therapy" is the exact edge the guidance flips on.

The **third** — the technologist's — is "where is the hard part, or is this a wrapper?" **T5 answers it best** (calibrated abstention, household-phone linkage) and T2 worst (an LLM with a barrier taxonomy). The chair's point: T1 is the only thesis that can *survive* all three, provided it fixes the first by conceding it on stage. A team that says "the doctor types fourteen fields today and that is wrong; the nurse takes the first nine" wins the room in that sentence.

### 4. How does each thesis differentiate from RetinaX, Setu, CareWeave, RAD-SMART and oncobrief — and what will a judge say out loud?

- **T1** — the judge says: **"This is RetinaX for lab reports."** It is the most dangerous comparison in the room because RetinaX is the diabetes-track team and already uses the care-loop language with ASHA re-engagement. The answer is a different leak, a different owner and a different artefact: RetinaX initiates an eye journey and reminds; T1 receives someone else's report and proves an obligation closed, with a public receipt the patient can verify. Against **Setu**, if the team shows the escalation lane, the judge says **"so you're Setu"** — which is why the escalation must be a fallback shown for eight seconds, never the second act. And the team should say the quiet part: RetinaX's public repo admits its reminders are not actually sent, so a working refusal beats a claimed loop.
- **T2** — the judge says: **"Setu is this, for cancer, with the same barrier taxonomy."** Then, worse: **"Yuktii gives this free and Clinizy bundles it for ₹1,499."** T2 has no answer that fits in a breath.
- **T3** — the judge says: **"RetinaX does the eye loop; you have done the same thing with five more specialities"** and, from the government judge, **"this is an NPCDCS register with better typography."**
- **T4** — the judge says: **"CareWeave already builds a source-linked timeline; you are the same thing from a plastic bag."** And from the technologist: **"extraction is bundled free in Eka and HealthPlix."**
- **T5** — no one in the programme does it, which is its strength and its trap. The judge says: **"So it is a matching engine. Why is that a company and not a feature of the other four?"** The honest answer — that it is the invisible failure underneath all four — is also the reason it should sit inside T1 rather than on a stage.

### 5. What is the demo failure that would sink each thesis on stage, and what is the fallback?

- **T1** — a judge has already clicked the seeded critical and closed it, so the hero screen shows zero unclosed and the story dies in the first ten seconds; or the refusal renders as a generic validation toast and reads as form-filling rather than a control. **Fallback:** a second untouched receipt, the public verifier tab which needs no login, and the 47-second captioned video. Rehearse the opening on a *dirty* database, not a clean one.
- **T2** — the judge asks "is that actually sending?" and the answer is no, and the whole claim goes with it. Secondary: Devanagari or Roman Hindi rendering badly on the venue projector. **Fallback:** say it plainly — "nothing is sent in this build; this is the clerk's worklist and script" — then show the ledger and the unreached lane, and play the recorded thread.
- **T3** — the pack cannot be completed live, because completion needs another provider, so the team mimes a stamp and the judge sees a mock. **Fallback:** one fully stamped pack in the fixture and a ten-second QR stamp on a second phone.
- **T4** — live OCR on an unseen document mislabels a drug, and a doctor in the room recognises the error; you do not recover from that. **Fallback:** never OCR cold; use the pre-scanned bag plus a deliberate "we refused this page" panel.
- **T5** — there is no screen, so the team narrates a queue and the technologist asks "show me" and receives a table. **Fallback:** twenty seconds of two-Sunita abstention, then concede the point and fold it into T1.

### 6. What does the two-page brief and the 7-minute video need to lead with?

Both must lead with **one named synthetic patient and one dated failure**, then the artefact, then the KPI, then the limit. Not TAM, not architecture, not the team, not 101 million.

- **Page 1, first line:** the incident — "K 6.1, flagged 08:50, nobody's name on it, closed six hours later." Then who, then what changes, then the one screenshot.
- **Page 2, first line:** the regulatory sentence, before the business model. Then who pays and how much, the KPI with its baseline or a "to be measured" label, the 60–90 day pilot, and the kill criterion. A judge who reads only the top of each page should be able to repeat the thesis.
- **Video 0:00–0:20:** the doctor's face, not the UI. "I did not see this." The product should not appear before 0:40, and the refusal must appear before 4:00 — earlier than feels comfortable, because judges decide by minute three and spend the rest confirming.
- **Last twenty seconds of both:** the artefact in a hand — certificate, pack, one-pager — and the ask in one sentence. No summary slide. The final image is the thing they will describe.

One constraint worth obeying: the brief must survive a 90-second skim by two doctors. That means one table, one screenshot, no paragraph longer than three lines.

### 7. Which thesis is most likely to be seen as "another dashboard", and how does it escape?

**T3 is structurally a dashboard** — bundle-completion rate, coverage percentage, a gap list per patient-year — and it cannot fully escape, because its unit is the patient-year and there is no action a doctor can take on it in forty seconds. Its only escape is to make the paper pack the hero and never show a rate without a named patient behind it, which is a real but partial fix.

**T1 is the one at risk by accident**, and it is the more interesting case: its landing page is `/clinic/results` with six statistic tiles, and a nervous presenter will open there. The escape is a written rule, not a redesign — the opening frame is *one obligation, one name, one clock*; the second frame is the refusal; the words "dashboard", "analytics" and "insights" never leave anyone's mouth; and the stats strip appears once, at the end, as proof that the list has a denominator. T2 reads as a CRM rather than a dashboard, which is a different insult; T4 is one page, so it is safe; T5 has no dashboard because it has no screen.

### 8. Which thesis would I award first prize to, and which would I shortlist but not award?

**Award T1.** Take T1 to the finale, and take it as the receipt, not as the accreditation product. It is the only thesis that produces a moment, an artefact, a KPI, a limit and a regulatory sentence in the same four minutes, and the only one whose demo gets *better* when the venue misbehaves.

**Shortlist T2, do not award it.** It is a genuine contender — best fit to the bar's letter, cleanest buyer arithmetic, strongest India scale, and a KPI a judge can restate — and it should be the fallback thesis if the receipt's adoption risk proves fatal in the concierge test. It should not be the pitch on 28 November, because a judge will say "Setu" out loud and the room will nod.

**T5 is the one I would fund and not prize.** It is the best technology here and the weakest stage; it belongs inside T1 as a feature (which is where the current build already puts it), and as a standalone company it is a 2027 conversation with a lab chain, not a 2026 hackathon pitch. **T4** I would shortlist only if the doctor partner personally commits to using it, because its only real distribution is a doctor's own conviction. **T3** I would not shortlist at this stage: it needs partners it does not have and a year it does not have.
