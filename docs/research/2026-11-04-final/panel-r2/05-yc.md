## 2. What is the hair-on-fire problem, in the founder's own words? Vitamin or painkiller?

The docs have the sentence, and it is good: *"Nobody in Indian healthcare is paid to close a loop. Every rupee is earned opening an obligation — a test, a visit, a referral, a prescription — and none is earned discharging it."*

That is the real insight, and it is a founder's insight, not a consultant's. Keep it.

But the answer to your question is uncomfortable. **It is a painkiller for the liability carrier and a vitamin for everyone else.** The patient feels the pain and never pays. The doctor carries the blame and will not pay for a screen. The lab's liability ends at delivery. Only the quality head has both the pain and the chequebook — and a quality head's pain spikes in the sixty days before a survey, then goes quiet for ten months.

So the honest shape is: an acute painkiller, seasonally triggered, sold to one person. That is a good business. It is not "hair on fire" in the sense that someone is calling you on a Sunday. Nobody has called. Ten interviews is what converts this sentence from literature into a fact.

## 3. Who is the buyer, what budget line, and what do they stop paying for?

**Buyer:** quality head or medical superintendent, 50–200 bed NABH-accredited private hospital. Secondary: a lab's quality manager. The doctor is the user, not the payer — that is the single best structural decision in the strategy.

**Budget line:** accreditation and quality. In practice one of three: the accreditation consultant's retainer (₹50k–2L per cycle, renewed at every survey — the best target because it has a date), the LIMS renewal (which will bundle critical alerts free to keep the account), or MRD staff time (which will not be displaced in year one).

**What they stop paying for: nothing, yet.** This is the weakest joint in the whole plan and the documents never name it. A ₹12,000/month product that is a *new* line item in a hospital is a much harder sale than one that replaces a line. The only disciplined answer is "it is cheaper than one adverse finding" — and that must be tested with a purchase order, not an LOI. The plan already says the right thing elsewhere ("a ₹1 deposit is a yes"). Hold them to it.

## 4. What is the wedge, and what is the expansion? Is the wedge small enough to dominate?

**The strategy's wedge is right:** abnormal lab results in diabetes, one city, one lab partner, one language pair, ten hospitals. Small enough to dominate, frequent enough to matter, dated, and currently unowned.

**The shipped product's wedge is too wide.** Four roles, nine nav sections, a register, a certificate, a slip, a replay and a legacy ledger. A first sale that asks a hospital to change four people's workflows at once is a system install, not a wedge.

**The wedge I would actually sell:** critical potassium and sodium, one lab, one clinic chain, a 60-minute SLA. The lab pays ₹0.30 a sample and prints the code. The clinic only has to tap once. No desk, no register, no certificate in the first contract.

**Expansion:** recheck and referral obligations on the same state machine, then the annual review pack, then the receipt standard across labs. The docs have this sequence right. The discipline to preserve is that expansion reuses the same buyer relationship — do not let the certificate become a second product sold to a second person.

## 5. Do things that don't scale: the first ten-customer concierge version, and what it proves

Ten clinics in one city, one lab feeding all ten, four weeks, founder-run. Per clinic, per week:

1. The lab WhatsApps the week's abnormal PDFs to the founder's phone.
2. The founder types six fields by hand into a sheet. No parser. No ingestion claim.
3. At 08:45 the founder sends the doctor **one WhatsApp line per critical** — value, flag, patient, minutes to breach.
4. The nurse calls, replies with the read-back in the same thread. The founder logs it with a timestamp.
5. Friday: the founder prints the register from a template and hands it over.

**What it proves, in order of value:** (a) whether a doctor acts on a WhatsApp line at all — the cheapest possible test of the one-tap thesis, and the one that kills the product if it fails; (b) the true baseline handoff lag on paper today; (c) whether read-back happens unprompted; (d) the autonomy ratio when the founder leaves for one week.

**What it does not prove:** that anyone pays. Ten clinics on a free concierge is ten favours. The tenth invoice is the experiment.

## 6. Default-alive math, using the documents' numbers

The documents say: default alive at ₹5.25 lakh/month = **53 hospitals at ₹10,000** or **27 at ₹20,000**, against ~4,000 NABH hospitals (1.3%).

The arithmetic holds: 53 × 10,000 = ₹5.3L; 27 × 20,000 = ₹5.4L. Three problems with it:

1. **The payback does not reconcile.** ₹12,000/month at 78% gross margin is ₹9,360 of margin. At the stated ₹60,000 CAC that is **6.4 months**, not the stated 5. Either CAC is ₹45,000 or payback is 6.4 months. Fix the line; a judge who divides will find it.
2. **Fifty-three hospitals is not a state, it is a two-year sales build.** At ₹60,000 CAC that is ₹31.8 lakh of sales spend before breakeven, plus the team's burn — which the documents never state. "Default alive at ₹5.25 lakh/month" silently assumes a ₹5.25 lakh/month cost base, which is a two-person company, not one building four roles and a lab channel.
3. **The lab line is the only one that reaches default alive without a salesperson per clinic** — one contract at 50M samples × ₹0.30 = ₹1.5 crore/year — and it has had zero conversations. So the default-alive plan is either a slow grind or an untested phone call. Say that on stage; do not imply the 53-hospital path is near.

## 7. The assumption that kills the company if false — and the cheapest test this month

**The assumption:** *a quality head will pay from her own budget to close the loop, not merely ask for a report.*

Not the baseline rate — the Indian literature already suggests that is bad (25.77% of criticals reaching a clinician). The unsolved half is willingness to pay in the absence of an incident.

**The test, this month, for ₹0:** ten interviews in ten days, with the app closed, ending in a concrete ask — ₹10,000/month, a PO or a ₹1 deposit. The protocol in `06-VALIDATION-PROTOCOL.md §3` is already correct; the flaw is that it has not been run. One paid invoice from one clinic converts this company from a proposal into a business. Zero from ten kills it. Both answers are worth having before 28 November.

Second-cheapest, two weeks: ask one lab whether it will print the verification code and pay ₹0.30 a sample. A yes makes the venture case; a no reduces this to a good niche SaaS, which is still worth building but is a different pitch.

## 8. Right path, or change the thesis before the finale?

**Right path. Do not touch the thesis.** The insight is correct, the unit is correct, the buyer is correctly identified, and the certificate is genuinely novel. Changing thesis again — this would be the fourth pivot in this project — would be fatal. The previous panel said it: churn after 28 November is death, and churn *before* it has a cost too.

**Change three things, all operability, none strategy:**

1. **Reseed the line and make the demo non-consumable.** The flagship screen is a resource with no reset. A prior session tapped `Seen` and the queue emptied; the e2e suite asserts `count > 0` after seeding, which proves the seed *can* produce cards. Ship a standing queue of eight pending results and a reseed control. This alone is worth ~10 points of demo readiness.
2. **Gate the four role homes by capability, not by nav.** A reviewer reading the whole register by URL is the first thing a technical judge will try. It is a one-day fix and it defends the central claim.
3. **Seed one genuine identity hold.** The refusal is the moment the room remembers, and the data currently shows a 100% match. Two candidates at 0.6 is a five-row change to a fixture.

**Then get one paid invoice and one lab's name in the ask.** A receipt is proof of a promise; an invoice is proof of a company.

## 10. Also asked by the packet

**Strongest moment.** The closure certificate. A surveyor-facing page that lists four missing evidence rows instead of claiming compliance, and stamps "not externally anchored yet" under its own hash. That is the only screen in this competition that could make a hospital quality head lean forward.

**First thing a judge will break.** The role gate. Sign in as **Reviewer**, type `/clinic/register`, read every patient and value. It survives as a rendering; it does not survive as a claim of role separation.

**Three fixes, in order.** (1) Seed a standing line queue plus a reseed control. (2) Gate routes by capability. (3) Seed one honest 0.6 identity hold, and put the facility header and printed-at on the register.

**Would I use it in my clinic tomorrow?** No — because the doctor's screen would be empty on arrival and my quality officer would ask why the register has no hospital name on it. Both are half-day fixes, which is exactly why they are unforgivable.

**Verdict.** The best-built, most honest product in this track, sitting behind a blank first screen and zero paid customers — fix the seed, gate the roles, and come back with one invoice, and this wins.
